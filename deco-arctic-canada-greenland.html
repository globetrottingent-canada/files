Décor & Party Guide

Arctic Canada & Greenland

The warm glow of a qulliq lamp against endless white, bannock shared by firelight, and the Northern Lights turning overhead.

01

The Visual Spirit

This is the oldest continuous culture in the Americas — 5,000 years of Inuit (and Kalaallit, in Greenland) ecological knowledge built for survival and community at the edge of the habitable world. Rather than a costume theme, the honest approach is warmth, generosity, and quiet respect: the golden glow of a qulliq (traditional oil lamp) against the endless white of the tundra, bannock shared freely among everyone present, and the Northern Lights turning slowly overhead. Sharing itself is central to the culture here — successful hunters traditionally distribute their catch through the whole community, and food is identity and medicine as much as sustenance.

Greenland layers on a Danish colonial thread — smørrebrød, pastry, and coffee culture alongside traditional Kalaallit food — giving the region two related but distinct registers to draw from. The palette across both is aurora green and ice blue, qulliq-flame amber, snow white, and deep polar-night navy.

A note on respect

Throat singing (katajjaq) and drum dancing are living, UNESCO-recognized art forms with real ceremonial and spiritual roots — throat singing in particular was historically suppressed and has been proudly revived by Inuit communities. Appreciate genuine performances and recordings rather than attempting to imitate them as party entertainment. Likewise, traditional clothing like the amauti (parka) and kamiik (sealskin boots) are functional heritage garments, not costumes — a warm coat or parka nods to the practical reality of the climate without needing to reproduce specific traditional dress.

02

Setting the Scene

Keep the light warm, low, and golden — a qulliq-style oil lamp or simply candlelight against a white, icy backdrop captures the mood better than anything bright or garish. Bring in soft furs and wool for texture, deep blues and greens for the aurora, and let bannock (the fry-bread now considered thoroughly Indigenous, despite its Scottish fur-trade origins) anchor the food table alongside warming soups and stews.

For a Greenlandic evening, add a Danish layer: smørrebrød (open sandwiches), pastries, and good coffee alongside the country-food table. Either way, emphasize sharing and communal warmth over spectacle — a big pot everyone eats from says more about this region than any single decoration could.

03

Dressing the Room

  • Light the room with a qulliq-style lamp or candlelight — warm, low, and golden against the cold.
  • Bring in aurora colour — soft greens and blues, perhaps as fabric drapes or lighting, evoking the Northern Lights.
  • Use faux fur and wool throws for texture and warmth — practical materials genuinely suited to the climate.
  • Set out bannock as a table centrepiece — the bread that's become thoroughly woven into Indigenous Arctic food culture.
  • Add a communal serving pot for stew or soup — sharing, not individual plating, is the region's true spirit.
  • (Greenland) add a smørrebrød and pastry spread for the Danish-influenced layer of modern Nuuk.
  • Keep snow-white and deep-navy as the base palette, with aurora colour as the accent.
  • Appreciate Inuit art — carvings, prints, and textiles — as genuine cultural expression worth honouring, ideally sourced from Indigenous artists.

05

Did You Know?

  • The qulliq, a traditional oil lamp carved from soapstone, provided light, heat, and cooking for Arctic peoples for millennia — it holds such cultural significance that it's featured on Nunavut's official coat of arms and is still used ceremonially today.
  • Inuit throat singing (katajjaq) is traditionally a playful duet between two women, face to face, testing who can outlast the other — it was suppressed for a time but has been proudly revived, and artists like Tanya Tagaq have brought it to international audiences.
  • Greenlandic Inuit drum dancing — performed with a frame drum called a qilaat — was recognised by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2021, honouring its role in community identity, storytelling, and continuity between generations.
  • Bannock, introduced by Scottish fur traders in the 18th century, is now considered a traditional First Nations and Inuit food — one of food history's rare examples of an introduced dish being fully absorbed into Indigenous identity.
Décor & Ambiance Reference

The pieces, warmth, and sound of an authentic Arctic gathering.

06

Room & Table Décor

Country Food & Warmth

  1. A warm, low-flame light source echoing the traditional soapstone oil lamp — the golden centrepiece of Arctic warmth and gathering.
  2. Fried or baked bannock, served for everyone to share — the comfort food that bridges introduced and Indigenous Arctic cooking.
  3. A big shared pot of stew or soup, echoing the region's deep cultural value of communal sharing over individual portions.
  4. Soft fur throws and wool blankets — practical, cosy materials genuinely suited to the Arctic climate.

Aurora & Greenland

  1. Soft green-and-blue lighting or drapery evoking the aurora borealis — a genuinely magical, non-costumed way to set the mood.
  2. Cloudberry jam or crowberry preserves — the genuine seasonal sweetness of the brief Arctic summer.
  3. Open-faced Danish-style sandwiches — a nod to Greenland's Danish colonial food heritage, alongside traditional Kalaallit dishes.
  4. Inuit carvings, prints, or textiles, ideally sourced from Indigenous artists — genuine, meaningful cultural expression as décor.
07

What to Wear

Practical & Warm

  1. A warm, practical coat nods honestly to the climate without reproducing specific traditional Inuit dress like the amauti.
  2. Cosy wool sweaters and layers — comfortable, simple, and genuinely climate-appropriate.
  3. Simple knit mittens and scarves — practical winter dress that fits the mood without appropriating specific heritage garments.

Greenlandic Touch

  1. Simple Scandinavian-style patterned knitwear — a nod to Greenland's Danish colonial layer.
  2. Soft greys, whites, and navy — understated, practical, and true to the region's aesthetic restraint.
08

Music & Sound

Inuit & Circumpolar

  1. Genuine recordings of Inuit throat singing (artists like Tanya Tagaq or PIQSIQ) — a powerful, living art form best enjoyed as the real thing rather than imitated.
  2. Recordings of traditional Greenlandic drum dancing and song — storytelling music with deep community meaning.
  3. Modern Inuit musicians blending traditional elements with contemporary sound — a genuine, living bridge between old and new.

Ambient & Greenlandic

  1. Soft ambient sound — wind, ice, and quiet — evoking the vast, still Arctic landscape as a gentle backdrop.
  2. Gentle Scandinavian folk music — a nod to Greenland's Danish colonial cultural thread.
