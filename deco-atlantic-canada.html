Décor & Party Guide

Atlantic Canada

A wharf-side lobster boil, a kitchen party with the fiddle going, and Newfoundland's own wild welcome — the screech-in.

01

The Visual Spirit

Atlantic Canada is built on the sea and on gathering — Mi'kmaq, Acadian, and British-Irish-Scottish maritime traditions layered around the world's richest cold-water fishing grounds. The everyday register is wharf and lobster-boil culture: a picnic table outdoors, a lobster cracked open with bare hands, butter running down your wrist, and a cold local beer. Alongside it runs the kitchen party — the ceilidh — where fiddle, accordion, and piano turn any room into a dance floor, a Celtic-Acadian inheritance especially strong in Cape Breton, PEI, and rural New Brunswick.

Newfoundland stands apart entirely — an independent dominion until 1949, with its own dialect, its own dishes, and its own boisterous welcome ceremony for outsiders (the screech-in). The palette across the region runs to wharf red and weathered wood, lobster-shell red, deep Atlantic blue, and dulse purple, with rope, buoys, and salt air throughout.

02

Setting the Scene

For a Maritime lobster boil, set up outdoors if you can — a picnic table, butter and crackers, and lobster boiled properly (in seawater, if you're lucky enough to have it). Add wharf touches: rope, buoys, and weathered wood, plus a cold local beer bar (Keith's or a regional craft brewer).

For a kitchen party (ceilidh), clear space for dancing and bring in real instruments — fiddle, accordion, guitar — even if it's just for guests to try. This is the heart of Maritime hospitality: informal, musical, and warmly inclusive.

For a Newfoundland-themed night, the screech-in is the obvious centrepiece — a shot of Screech rum, a traditional phrase to recite, and (with a wink, and perhaps a rubber stand-in rather than the real thing) the famous "kiss the cod." Add a sou'wester rain hat as a prop, and finish with a certificate for the newly "screeched in."

03

Dressing the Room

  • Set up a lobster boil — outdoors if possible, with butter, crackers, and a cold local beer.
  • Bring in wharf touches — rope, buoys, and weathered wood for genuine East Coast texture.
  • Clear room for a kitchen party — fiddle, accordion, and dancing are the true heart of Maritime hospitality.
  • Set up a screech-in station for a Newfoundland theme — Screech rum, a recitation card, and a wink toward "kissing the cod."
  • Add a sou'wester rain hat as a fun prop — the classic fisherman's gear associated with the screech-in ceremony.
  • Serve dulse (dried purple seaweed) as a genuinely local, distinctive snack.
  • Bring in Acadian colour — the blue-white-red tricolour with its gold star — for a nod to the region's French heritage.
  • Keep it warm and unpretentious — the whole spirit of Atlantic Canadian hospitality is generous, not fussy.

05

Did You Know?

  • The screech-in ceremony — reciting a phrase, taking a shot of Screech rum, and kissing a cod — welcomes "come from aways" (outsiders) as honorary Newfoundlanders, most famously along George Street in St. John's, with a certificate to prove it.
  • A ceilidh (pronounced "kay-lee") is an old-school kitchen party found across Nova Scotia, PEI, and New Brunswick — fiddle, piano, bagpipes, and guitar, with roots in the region's Scottish, Irish, and Acadian heritage. Cape Breton's Celtic Colours festival celebrates it every October.
  • PEI produces roughly 25% of Canada's entire potato crop despite being the smallest province — the potato appears on the province's licence plates, that's how central it is to island identity.
  • Newfoundland was an independent dominion, not a Canadian province, until 1949 — the most recent addition to Canada, and a large part of why its food and dialect remain so distinctly its own.
Décor & Ambiance Reference

The pieces, the dress, and the sound of an authentic East Coast gathering.

06

Room & Table Décor

Wharf & Kitchen Party

  1. Whole lobster, melted butter, and crackers — the defining Atlantic Canadian summer meal, best eaten outdoors with bare hands.
  2. Coiled rope, colourful buoys, and salt-worn wood — the genuine visual language of a Maritime fishing wharf.
  3. Real instruments set out for guests to play — the heart of a ceilidh, and an invitation to spontaneous music.
  4. Dried purple dulse seaweed, salty and chewy — the region's single most distinctive snack food.

Newfoundland & Acadian

  1. Screech rum, a recitation card, and a wink toward the famous "kiss the cod" — Newfoundland's playful honorary-islander tradition.
  2. The classic yellow fisherman's rain hat, often worn by the "master screecher" leading the ceremony.
  3. The Acadian flag — the French tricolour with a gold star — a proud marker of the region's Francophone heritage.
  4. Terracotta-red accents nodding to Prince Edward Island's famous iron-rich soil and its beloved potatoes.
07

What to Wear

Wharf & Kitchen Party

  1. Warm flannel or wool layers — the practical, unpretentious everyday look of the East Coast.
  2. Simple rubber boots — practical, genuine, and right at home on a working wharf.

Newfoundland

  1. Full fisherman's rain gear — the classic "master screecher" costume for a Newfoundland-themed screech-in.
  2. A warm wool sweater — comfortable, genuine, and suited to Newfoundland's cool, foggy climate.
08

Music & Sound

Ceilidh & Celtic

  1. Fast, driving fiddle music rooted in Scottish and Irish heritage — the defining sound of a Maritime kitchen party.
  2. French-language folk music carrying the Acadian story — resilient, warm, and distinct from Québécois sound.
  3. Beloved East Coast folk songs that everyone at a kitchen party knows the words to — communal by design.

Newfoundland

  1. Classic Newfoundland folk songs, often played loudly during a screech-in — rowdy, proud, and utterly of the island.
  2. Newfoundland's most beloved modern folk-rock export — an easy, joyful way to bring island energy to any playlist.
