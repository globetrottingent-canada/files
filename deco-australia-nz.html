Décor & Party Guide

Australia & New Zealand

Backyard barbie warmth and outback red, the powerful stamp of a haka, and a hāngi feast — two nations, close neighbours, distinct heritage.

01

The Visual Spirit

Australia and New Zealand are close neighbours, linked by the ANZAC bond and the Southern Cross on both flags, but each carries its own distinct heritage. Australia is backyard-barbie warmth, sunburnt outback red, beach culture, and bushwear (Akubra hats, Driza-Bone coats) — relaxed, egalitarian, and a little cheeky. New Zealand carries a deep, living Māori culture alongside its European heritage — the haka's thunderous power, the hāngi earth-oven feast, and Kiwi beach-Christmas warmth (summer Down Under flips the whole calendar).

Both nations share genuine ANZAC Day reverence and a laid-back, outdoorsy sensibility, so a shared party can draw on either or both — sunburnt-country red and gum-leaf green for Australia, deep green and Māori-inspired pattern for New Zealand — with plenty of natural material, silver fern, and open-air warmth throughout.

A note on Aboriginal and Māori culture

Aboriginal dot painting, the didgeridoo, and the boomerang carry deep ceremonial and spiritual meaning for Australia's First Nations peoples — appreciate them as living heritage rather than casual costume or novelty props. Likewise, the haka and Māori design (tā moko, kōwhaiwhai patterns) are sacred and specific — enjoy a genuine performance or authentic craft, but avoid imitating ceremonial elements yourself. Both cultures are proudly, visibly part of national identity; the ask is simply respect over caricature.

02

Setting the Scene

For an Aussie backyard barbie, fire up the grill as the centrepiece, bring in outback red-and-ochre tones, gum leaves and native bottlebrush flowers, and set out a casual spread — snags, prawns (never called "shrimp"), and a proper Esky full of cold drinks. Bushwear touches (an Akubra hat, corrugated-iron accents) and a relaxed, no-fuss table finish the look.

For a New Zealand gathering, lean into deep native green, silver fern motifs, and — if the occasion calls for it — a proper hāngi-style feast (or a nod to one, with foods cooked "underground-style" in foil). Keep the mood warm, communal, and outdoorsy; New Zealanders famously spend Christmas at the beach, so a summery, alfresco table works for almost any Kiwi celebration.

Dressing the Room

  • Australia: fire up the barbie as the centrepiece — snags, prawns, and steak, casual and unfussy.

  • Australia: add bushwear touches — an Akubra hat, a Driza-Bone coat, corrugated-iron or verandah styling.
  • New Zealand: lean into deep native green and the silver fern motif — New Zealand's most recognisable national symbol.
  • New Zealand: set up a hāngi-style feast (or a foil-wrapped nod to one) for a genuine, communal centrepiece.
  • Both: fly the Southern Cross — present on both flags and a shared symbol of the Southern Hemisphere sky.
  • Both: keep it outdoorsy and relaxed — beach culture, alfresco tables, and easy hospitality run through both nations.
  • (ANZAC Day) keep décor simple and reverent — rosemary sprigs and quiet remembrance rather than festive colour.

05

Did You Know?

  • ANZAC Day (April 25) is shared, solemn ground for both nations — commemorating the 1915 Gallipoli landing, marked by dawn services in both countries and by ANZAC biscuits (oats, coconut, golden syrup), originally baked and sent to soldiers by their families.
  • The didgeridoo is over 1,500 years old, one of the world's oldest instruments, and Aboriginal rock art in Arnhem Land dates back more than 20,000 years — Aboriginal culture is Earth's oldest continuing living culture.
  • The haka is far more than a rugby pre-match ritual — it's a traditional Māori posture dance used to express a range of emotions, from welcome to challenge, and remains a powerful, living cultural practice.
  • New Zealand's Waitangi Day (Feb 6) marks the 1840 signing of the Treaty of Waitangi between the British Crown and Māori chiefs — the country's founding document and an occasion for both celebration and reflection.
Décor & Ambiance Reference

Two nations, two distinct heritages — Aussie backyard warmth and Kiwi green.

06

Room & Table Décor

Australia

  1. A backyard grill loaded with snags, prawns, and steak — simple, fresh, and the true centre of Australian hospitality.
  2. Eucalyptus gum leaves and native bottlebrush or wattle flowers — an easy, genuine Australian botanical touch.
  3. An Akubra hat or Driza-Bone coat as decoration — iconic outback bushwear brands recognisable across the country.
  4. A cooler ("Esky") of cold drinks and a relaxed, unfussy table — Australian hosting is famously easygoing rather than formal.

New Zealand

  1. New Zealand's most iconic native plant motif — used everywhere from sports jerseys to décor, instantly recognisable as Kiwi.
  2. A nod to the traditional Māori method of cooking with heated stones in a pit oven — communal, smoky, and central to special occasions.
  3. New Zealand jade, deeply valued in Māori culture — best appreciated as genuine craft or jewellery rather than imitation.
  4. Light, alfresco, beach-adjacent styling — since Kiwi summer holidays (including Christmas) happen in warm weather, not snow.
07

What to Wear

Australia

  1. The classic wide-brimmed Australian bush hat — practical, iconic, and instantly recognisable.
  2. The waxed-canvas riding coat, a genuine piece of Australian rural heritage — practical and full of character.
  3. Relaxed beachwear or surf-brand casual — Australian style leans laid-back rather than formal.

New Zealand

  1. A silver fern motif worked into clothing or accessories — proud, simple national pride without overreach.
  2. Jandals (flip-flops) and beach casual — reflecting New Zealand's relaxed, outdoorsy everyday style.
  3. New Zealand's famous merino wool, in a simple sweater or wrap — soft, high-quality, and genuinely Kiwi-made.
08

Music & Sound

Australia

  1. Classic Australian rock and pub-friendly singalong tracks — the easy, upbeat soundtrack of a backyard barbie.
  2. One of the world's oldest instruments, with deep ceremonial roots — best enjoyed through a genuine recording or performance rather than attempted as a party novelty.
  3. Australian folk ballads like "Waltzing Matilda" — gentle, nostalgic, and deeply woven into national identity.

New Zealand

  1. Traditional and contemporary Māori song — melodic, meaningful, and a genuine window into living culture.
  2. New Zealand's strong contemporary music scene — an easy, upbeat addition to any modern Kiwi gathering.
  3. A powerful, meaningful posture dance — best appreciated by watching a genuine performance (in person or recorded) rather than attempting it as party entertainment.
