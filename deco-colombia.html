Décor & Party Guide

Colombia

A carnival of regions — cumbia and vallenato, towering flower arrangements, Wayuu weaving, and the masked joy of Barranquilla's Battle of Flowers.

01

The Visual Spirit

Colombia is a country of striking regional variety, and a Colombian party can honestly draw on any of them: the Caribbean coast's Carnaval de Barranquilla, with its masked characters and cumbia rhythm; Medellín's Feria de las Flores, where farmers carry towering flower arrangements on their backs; the coffee-growing Eje Cafetero's paisa culture, all sombreros and leather satchels; and the vallenato accordion tradition of the northern plains. Threaded through all of it is extraordinary craft — Wayuu woven bags, the iconic sombrero vueltiao — and a genuine, contagious love of colour and celebration.

The palette draws from the flag (yellow, blue, red) and from flowers in every possible hue, set against natural fibre, straw, and warm wood. Think masks and streamers for Carnaval energy, or flowers and woven textiles for a gentler, craft-forward version — either reads as authentically Colombian.

02

Setting the Scene

For a Carnaval-style party, go maximal: masks (a long-nosed Marimonda mask is instantly recognisable), streamers, bright costumes, and a "Battle of Flowers" table centrepiece piled with blooms. Add drums and wind instruments for the cumbia/porro sound, and don't forget a bit of flour or foam for a cheeky nod to Carnaval's messier traditions.

For a gentler, craft-forward party, build the table around towering flower arrangements in the style of Medellín's silleteros, bring in Wayuu-patterned textiles and mochila bags, and hang a sombrero vueltiao or two as decoration. Either way, keep coffee and a proper Colombian brew station close at hand, and leave room for dancing — cumbia, vallenato, and salsa all belong on the playlist.

03

Dressing the Room

  • Pile the table with flowers — a nod to Medellín's Feria de las Flores and Barranquilla's Battle of Flowers, Colombia's two great floral traditions.
  • Hang Marimonda and Garabato masks — the iconic long-nosed and caped characters of Carnaval de Barranquilla — as wall art or costume pieces.
  • Bring in Wayuu-woven textiles — geometric-patterned mochila bags and fabric, genuine indigenous craft from La Guajira.
  • Add a sombrero vueltiao — the woven caña flecha hat that's a recognised national symbol — as decoration or headwear.
  • Set up a proper coffee station — Colombian coffee is a point of deep national pride.
  • String streamers and bright bunting for Carnaval energy, or keep it calmer with woven textiles and flowers for the craft-forward route.
  • Include a bit of paisa/arriero style — sombrero aguadeño, leather carriel satchel — for a coffee-region touch.
  • Leave room to dance — cumbia, vallenato, and salsa caleña are all essential to the mood.

05

Did You Know?

  • The Carnaval de Barranquilla is a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage event blending European, African, and Indigenous traditions in one festival — its dances alone include the Spanish paloteo, the African congo, and the Indigenous mico y micas.
  • Carnaval's Battle of Flowers parade is presided over by the Carnival Queen, and the festivities close on Tuesday with the poetic, symbolic burial of "Joselito Carnaval," mourning the end of the celebration.
  • At Feria de las Flores, silleteros — flower farmers — carry towering, elaborate arrangements on wooden saddles strapped to their backs, walking through Medellín's streets in a tradition that's become the city's proudest symbol.
  • Wayuu mochila bags, hand-woven by artisans of La Guajira, carry deep cultural meaning in their geometric patterns — each design can represent nature, memory, or personal identity, passed down through generations of weavers.

06

Room & Table Décor

Carnaval Energy

  1. The long-nosed, big-eared mask worn by one of Barranquilla Carnaval's most beloved characters — instantly recognisable and full of playful energy.
  2. The decorated cape and staff of the Garabato dancer, symbolising the struggle between life and death — a striking, meaningful Carnaval prop.
  3. Bright paper streamers and confetti in rainbow colour — simple, cheerful, and true to Carnaval's maximalist spirit.
  4. A generous, colourful flower arrangement as centrepiece — a nod to Carnaval's most important event, La Batalla de Flores.

Craft & Countryside

  1. A towering flower arrangement inspired by the silleteros of Medellín — a beautiful, genuinely Colombian centrepiece.
  2. Geometric-patterned handwoven bags and textiles from La Guajira's Wayuu artisans — genuine indigenous craft, rich with meaning.
  3. The woven caña flecha hat, a recognised national symbol — displayed as decoration or worn as part of the look.
  4. Colombian coffee, properly brewed — a point of deep national pride and a natural social hub.
07

What to Wear

For Her

  1. A wide, colourful skirt and adorned blouse, worn by cumbiamba dancers at Carnaval — festive, traditional, and made for movement.
  2. A crown or arrangement of bright flowers — an easy, beautiful nod to the Feria de las Flores tradition.
  3. A Wayuu mochila bag and woven jewellery — genuine, meaningful craft as everyday-wearable accent pieces.
  4. Simply leaning into bold, saturated colour — Carnaval's spirit is more about joy and brightness than any single garment.

For Him

  1. Rolled-up drill trousers, a long-sleeved shirt, sombrero aguadeño, leather carriel satchel, and espadrilles — the symbolic dress of the Eje Cafetero.
  2. Colombia's iconic woven hat — an easy, recognisable, and genuinely national accessory.
  3. The full long-nosed Marimonda look — bright, playful, and one of Carnaval's most beloved and photographed costumes.
  4. A light, breathable linen shirt in a bright colour — simple, comfortable, and right for the climate and mood.
08

Music & Sound

Coastal & Carnaval

  1. Colombia's most iconic rhythm, blending Indigenous, African, and Spanish roots — the predominant sound of Carnaval de Barranquilla.
  2. Accordion-driven music from the northern plains, built on storytelling lyrics — celebrated at its own Valledupar festival each year.
  3. Brass-and-drum relatives of cumbia, both central to the Carnaval de Barranquilla soundscape.
  4. A fast, athletic Afro-Colombian dance and drum rhythm — one of Carnaval's most electrifying performances.

Andean & Modern

  1. Cali's distinctively fast-footwork salsa style — the city proudly calls itself the "Salsa Capital of the World."
  2. Colombia's massive modern pop and reggaeton scene — an easy, upbeat way to keep a mixed crowd dancing.
  3. A graceful Andean folk genre played on guitar and tiple — a softer, more traditional counterpoint to the coastal rhythms.
  4. An Afro-Colombian dance genre from Cartagena's picó sound-system culture — energetic and distinctly regional.
