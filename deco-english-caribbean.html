CARIBBEAN / ANGLO-CARIBBEAN

Décor & Party Guide

English Caribbean

Island time — chattel-house colour, rum-shop warmth, cricket on the radio, and the beat of Junkanoo horns and a Crop Over tuk band.

01

The Visual Spirit

This is the wider English-speaking Caribbean — the Bahamas, Barbados, Antigua, Grenada, St. Lucia, Dominica, St. Vincent, and their neighbours (Jamaica and Trinidad & Tobago each get their own dedicated page, since they've grown large, distinct traditions of their own). What unites the rest is an island-time warmth: candy-painted wooden chattel houses, fretwork verandas, a rum shop on every corner, cricket as a shared religion, and a fish-fry on a Friday night that turns into the whole evening's party.

Colour and craft carry the design language — batik and shell jewellery, woven baskets, madras plaid, and a palette straight off a Caribbean beach: turquoise sea, hot-pink bougainvillea, sunshine yellow, and the deep green of palm. Music and dance sit at the centre of it, from the horn-and-cowbell riot of Junkanoo to a Crop Over tuk band marching down the street.

Which island?

This page covers the broader Anglo-Caribbean — the Bahamas, Barbados, and the Eastern Caribbean islands. Looking for Jamaica or Trinidad & Tobago specifically? Each has its own dedicated décor page, since reggae/Rastafari and Carnival/steelpan/soca have grown into their own distinct traditions.

Within this page, feel free to lean into one island's flavour — Junkanoo for the Bahamas, Crop Over for Barbados, madras and Creole dress for Grenada, St. Lucia, or Dominica — or blend the shared Anglo-Caribbean island-time feel.

02

Setting the Scene

Paint the room the way a chattel house is painted — bold, contrasting, candy-bright colour on every trim and shutter, with white fretwork detail if you can manage it. Set a rum bar as the social hub (Barbados is rum's birthplace), scatter woven baskets, batik cloth, and shell jewellery around, and let cricket — a match on in the background, a bat and stumps as props — set the relaxed, communal mood. String bunting or paper lanterns for a festival feel, and don't skimp on tropical flowers.

For a bigger production, lean into one island's signature festival: Junkanoo's cowbell-and-horn costume parade for the Bahamas, or a Crop Over "de road" street-party look — glitter, feathers, and a tuk band — for Barbados. For the Eastern Caribbean islands, madras plaid and the flowing Creole "wob dwiyet" dress bring in real heritage and colour.

03

Dressing the Room

  • Paint it like a chattel house — bold, contrasting colours with white fretwork trim, a hallmark of Bajan and wider island architecture.
  • Set up a rum bar — Barbados is the birthplace of rum, and a rum punch bar is the natural social hub of an island party.
  • Scatter batik, baskets, and shell jewellery — handcrafted island textures that read as genuinely Caribbean rather than generic beach party.
  • Bring in madras plaid — the colourful checked fabric that's part of national dress across Grenada, St. Lucia, Dominica, Antigua, and beyond — as table runners or bunting.
  • Add a cricket touch — a bat and stumps, a match playing quietly, or cricket-whites detail; it's genuinely a way of life across the islands.
  • (Junkanoo) go for cowbells, whistles, and crepe-paper fringe costumes — the Bahamian parade look.
  • (Crop Over) add glitter, feathers, and sugar-cane touches — the harvest-festival-turned-street-party of Barbados.
  • Pile on tropical flowers and palm — hibiscus, bougainvillea, and bird of paradise for lush island colour.

05

Did You Know?

  • Junkanoo is the Bahamas' biggest cultural event — an all-night parade of elaborate costumes, cowbells, goatskin drums, and whistles held on Boxing Day and New Year's Day, with roots reaching back to West Africa.
  • Crop Over began as a Barbadian celebration marking the end of the sugar-cane harvest — now a summer-long festival of calypso, soca, and the traditional drum-and-fife tuk bands, culminating in the "Grand Kadooment" street parade.
  • Rum shop culture is the backbone of Bajan life — as numerous as churches, and the place where Barbadians gather to talk, argue, and above all follow cricket. Barbados is widely credited as rum's birthplace.
  • Madras plaid headwraps historically carried real meaning — in the French Creole islands, the number of peaks tied into the fabric signalled a woman's relationship status, a coded language born under conditions where open speech was dangerous.

06

Room & Table Décor

Island Foundations

  1. Bold, contrasting paint colours with white fretwork trim — the cheerful, portable wooden houses found across Barbados and the wider Eastern Caribbean.
  2. The colourful checked cotton fabric woven into the national dress of St. Lucia, Dominica, Grenada, and Antigua — beautiful as table runners or bunting.
  3. Hand-dyed batik cloth and woven baskets — genuine island craft that brings texture without leaning on cliché.
  4. A bat and stumps, or a match quietly playing — cricket is genuinely central to Anglo-Caribbean identity and community life.

Festival Statement Pieces

  1. Elaborate costumes built from crepe paper, cardboard, and bright fringe — the Bahamian parade art form, worth recreating even in miniature.
  2. Cowbells, whistles, and goatskin drums as both décor and instruments — hand them out and the Junkanoo energy builds itself.
  3. Feathers, glitter, and sequins for the Crop Over "de road" look, plus a nod to sugar cane — the festival's harvest origins.
  4. Barbadian and island rums, rum punch, and tropical garnish — the natural centre of gravity for any Anglo-Caribbean gathering.
07

What to Wear

For Her

  1. The graceful, full-skirted national dress of Dominica (and the "Jip" of St. Lucia), built from madras plaid with lace trim and a folded headwrap — elegant heritage dress for the Eastern Caribbean islands.
  2. A folded madras-plaid headwrap — a beautiful, meaningful accent on its own, without needing the full formal dress.
  3. A colourful cotton sundress in tropical print — comfortable, breezy, and right for any Caribbean-island gathering.
  4. Shell jewellery and a straw hat or bag — simple, genuine island accents.

For Him

  1. A white shirt with a madras-plaid sash or waistcoat — the traditional men's counterpart to the Creole women's dress.
  2. A light, breezy linen shirt in a bright colour — the easy, universal Caribbean-island look.
  3. Classic cricket whites — a fun, genuinely Anglo-Caribbean costume choice, and a conversation starter in themselves.
  4. A woven straw hat — simple, comfortable, and right at home at a rum shop or beach party alike.
08

Music & Sound

Festival Sounds

  1. Goatskin drums, cowbells, horns, and whistles driving the Bahamian parade beat — pure percussive energy.
  2. Barbados's own drum-and-fife folk sound, heard at Crop Over — a distinctive blend of African and British military rhythms.
  3. Danceable soca and storytelling calypso, common to festivals across the wider Anglo-Caribbean, not just Trinidad.
  4. Steel drum arrangements of familiar songs — a warm, universally loved island sound for a relaxed gathering.

Everyday Island Sound

  1. Barbados's own reggae-influenced pop sound — an easy-going, lesser-known island genre worth discovering.
  2. A homemade sound built on a hand saw played with a screwdriver, plus accordion and drums — genuine Bahamian folk music.
  3. Reggae and dancehall cross island lines throughout the Anglo-Caribbean — a natural, easy addition to any playlist.
  4. A European-inherited square dance kept alive across the Eastern Caribbean, often danced in madras and Creole dress.
