CARIBBEAN / FRANCO-ANTILLES

Décor & Party Guide

French Caribbean

Créole colour with a French accent — madras and lace, the drum-and-song of bèlè, King Vaval's carnival, and rhum agricole poured for the toast.

01

The Visual Spirit

Martinique and Guadeloupe carry the Caribbean's colour and rhythm with a distinctly French thread woven through — Creole patois beside the French language, madras plaid beside French lace, rhum agricole (distilled straight from fresh cane juice, an AOC-protected specialty) beside café culture. The result is warm, musical, and a little bit elegant: think a colonial-era town square dressed for a fête, with drums, bright fabric, and flowers everywhere.

The signature sound is the bèlè drum and its answering call-and-response song, the root of biguine and, later, the 1980s global hit zouk. The signature sight is Carnival's King Vaval, paraded and mourned each year, and the flowing madras dress worn at the Fête des Cuisinières and other Creole celebrations. Lean into deep colonial reds and blues, tropical flowers, madras plaid, and rum, and the room reads as genuinely Franco-Antillean.

A note on Haiti

This page focuses on Martinique and Guadeloupe — French overseas territories with a shared, closely related culture. Haiti, though also French-Creole-speaking, has been independent since 1804 and has its own distinct history, religion (Vodou), and traditions (Kanaval, compas music) that deserve their own dedicated page rather than being folded in here.

02

Setting the Scene

Dress the table in madras plaid runners or a lace-trimmed white cloth, and bring in real tropical flowers — hibiscus, bird of paradise, anthurium — in abundance. Set up a rhum agricole bar (served the traditional way: a bottle, cane syrup, and lime, so guests build their own "ti punch"), and let bèlè or zouk play in the background. Add colonial-blue or deep-red accents, wicker and dark wood furniture, and a few pieces of French-Creole pottery or ironwork for texture.

For a bigger production, lean into Carnival: bright costumes, satirical "groupes à pied" energy, and King Vaval as a mock-monarch centrepiece — or the Fête des Cuisinières, with baskets of Creole delicacies, madras-and-lace "cook" outfits, and gwo ka drumming. Either way, keep the room ready to dance; the vidé (carnival street party) spirit is contagious.

03

Dressing the Room

  • Lay down madras plaid — as a table runner, bunting, or napkins — the defining Franco-Antillean fabric.
  • Set up a ti punch bar — rhum agricole, cane syrup, and lime, so guests mix their own the traditional way.
  • Bring in tropical flowers in abundance — hibiscus, anthurium, and bird of paradise, loose and generous.
  • Add colonial colour — deep blues and reds, wicker furniture, dark wood, and a touch of French ironwork or lace.
  • Bring a bèlè drum (or the image of one) as a centrepiece — the heartbeat instrument of Martinican and Guadeloupean music.
  • (Carnival) add a King Vaval figure — a satirical mock-monarch effigy — plus bright costumes and repainted "bradjak" car imagery for a cheeky touch.
  • (Fête des Cuisinières) fill baskets with Creole dishes and flowers, and dress a "cook" figure in madras, lace, and embroidered apron.
  • Leave room to dance — the vidé street-party spirit runs through every Franco-Antillean gathering.

Did You Know?

  • The bèlè drum — traditionally built from staves of old rum barrels topped with goatskin — anchors a whole world of song, storytelling, and dance, and gave rise to biguine, which later helped birth zouk in the 1980s.
  • King Vaval, Martinique's Carnival King, is a satirical effigy whose identity is kept secret until his grand reveal — he's ceremonially mourned and burned on Ash Wednesday to close the festivities.
  • The Fête des Cuisinières in Guadeloupe has run for over a century: women cooks parade in madras, lace, and embroidered aprons to have their baskets and utensils blessed at the cathedral, then share a great communal feast.
  • Rhum agricole is distilled directly from fresh-pressed sugarcane juice rather than molasses, giving it a grassier, more vegetal flavor — Martinique's rhum agricole even carries its own protected AOC designation, like French wine.

Room & Table Décor

Everyday Créole

  1. The signature checked fabric, used as runners, bunting, or napkins — instantly recognisable and rooted in real Creole heritage.
  2. Cane-juice rhum, cane syrup, and lime set out for guests to build their own ti punch — the traditional, participatory way to serve it.
  3. A goatskin-topped drum, traditionally built from rum-barrel staves — a striking centrepiece with real musical history behind it.
  4. Deep colonial blue accents, wicker furniture, dark wood, and wrought-iron detail — a nod to old Creole town architecture.

Carnival & Fête

  1. A satirical effigy figure, kept secret until his reveal and mourned at the close of Carnival — a fun, theatrical centrepiece.
  2. Bold, playful costume colour and mask elements — Martinique's Carnival is famous for cheeky, socially satirical character costumes.
  3. Baskets filled with Creole delicacies and flowers, styled after the ones carried and blessed at Guadeloupe's cooks' festival.
  4. Hibiscus, anthurium, and bird of paradise, arranged loose and abundant — the vivid colour note that ties the whole room together.
07

What to Wear

For Her

  1. A madras-plaid dress with lace and embroidery detail, in the style worn at the Fête des Cuisinières — festive, elegant heritage dress.
  2. A folded madras headwrap — a beautiful, meaningful Creole accessory that works with a simple outfit.
  3. Ornate Creole gold — chains, hoops, and traditional forms — a distinctive Franco-Antillean jewellery style.
  4. Bright, mismatched colour and a bit of mask or feather detail for a fun Carnival-inspired look.

For Him

  1. A madras-plaid shirt, or a plain white shirt with a madras sash — a simple, authentic touch of Creole colour.
  2. Light linen trousers and a woven straw hat — comfortable and effortlessly Franco-Antillean.
  3. A playful, satirical carnival costume — Martinique's Carnival characters (like the banana-leaf-covered "bear") are colourful and full of local wit.
  4. A ti punch in hand completes the character — rhum agricole is central to nearly every Franco-Antillean occasion.
08

Music & Sound

Roots

  1. Drum, song, and dance in a living call-and-response tradition — the deepest root of Martinican music, still celebrated at its own annual festival.
  2. Guadeloupe's foundational hand-drum tradition — the percussive heartbeat of the Fête des Cuisinières and countless other gatherings.
  3. Born in 1890s Saint-Pierre from Creolised mazurka and quadrille — elegant, syncopated, and the direct ancestor of zouk.
  4. European ballroom forms reworked with African syncopation — still danced at folkloric events across both islands.

Modern & Carnival

  1. Popularised worldwide by the Guadeloupean-Martinican group Kassav' in the 1980s — smooth, romantic, and instantly recognisable.
  2. A fast, participatory Carnival version of biguine, played on improvised drums — call-and-response energy built for the street parade.
  3. The harder, faster style of zouk, rooted in Carnival biguine rhythm — high-energy and built for the dance floor.
  4. Regional Caribbean crossover sounds heard at Carnival — a lively, contemporary complement to the traditional forms.
